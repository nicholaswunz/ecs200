Research Design

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code

Good analysis cannot rescue a poor design. Most of the problems you will meet in Weeks 5–13 (non-independent data, confounded treatments, too few replicates, false zeros) are much easier to prevent when you plan a study than to fix after the data are collected. This appendix adds to the lecture notes on research design, with a focus on designing studies that are easy to analyse.

From question to analysis

Plan your analysis before you collect data. Four principles for sound statistical ecology (Popovic et al., 2024):

  1. Define a focused research question, then plan the sampling and the analysis to answer it.
  2. Use a model that accounts for the distribution and dependence of your data (counts → Week 8, yes/no → Week 11, grouped or repeated measurements → Week 12).
  3. Emphasise effect sizes (how big is the effect, with a confidence interval) rather than only whether p < 0.05.
  4. Report your methods and results in enough detail that someone else could repeat the study and use your results (Davis and Kay, 2023; Gerstner et al., 2017).

A useful exercise: before collecting any data, sketch the figure you expect to make and write the R model formula you plan to fit, e.g. lm(sprint_speed ~ temperature * population). If you cannot write the formula, the design is not finished yet.

Who or what are you making conclusions about?

The scope of inference is the “population” your conclusions apply to. Define it in three dimensions before you sample:

  • Spatial: one wetland, all wetlands on the Swan Coastal Plain, or all of south-west WA?
  • Temporal: one breeding season, or long-term conditions? A question about a 10-year cycle needs more than one year of data.
  • Ecological: one species, one life stage, or a whole community?

Your sample can only represent the population you actually sampled from. Larger and more variable populations need more samples.

Replicate at the right scale

The most important design decision is the experimental (or sampling) unit: the smallest unit to which a treatment is independently applied, or that is independently sampled. Replication must happen at the same scale as the treatment (Hurlbert, 1984; Marshall, 2024).

Design Treatment applied to True replicate Pseudoreplicates (subsamples)
Nutrients added to individual pots Pot Pot Several leaves per plant
Water heated in a tank holding 5 snails Tank Tank The 5 snails
Fire applied to whole plots Plot Plot Quadrats within each plot
Comparing burnt and unburnt sites Site Site Traps within each site
Comparing high and low pH populations Population Population Individuals within a population

Subsamples are useful (they give a better estimate for each unit), but they are not independent replicates. Either average them for each experimental unit, or include the unit as a random effect (Week 12).

NoteLink to your project

On The Islands, each plot is the experimental unit for a nutrient treatment. Ten plants measured in one plot are not ten replicates. And if you compare islands, you have only one field station per island, so island and climate are completely confounded: any difference could be caused by climate, soil, or anything else that differs between the islands. Say so clearly in your report.

Randomise, intersperse and block

  • Randomise which units get which treatment, so unmeasured differences (soil, shade, observer) are spread evenly among treatments. Use R to do it:

  • Intersperse treatments in space and time. Putting all the controls in one row, or measuring all controls in the morning and all treatments in the afternoon, confounds the treatment with location or time.
  • Block when there is a known gradient (e.g. slope, distance from the coast). Group similar units into blocks and assign every treatment within each block. Block then becomes a random effect (Week 12) or a fixed factor in the analysis.

Controls and before–after designs

To measure the effect of an impact (mining, fire, restoration), you need to know what would have happened without it. Simple designs often give wrong answers: an analysis of hundreds of ecological datasets found that before–after or control–impact designs alone can be very inaccurate, and that before–after control–impact (BACI) and randomised designs were much more reliable (Christie et al., 2019; Christie et al., 2020). Where possible, collect data before the impact at both impact and control sites, and use several control sites. Experiments built into management actions are one of the best ways to get this evidence (Ockendon et al., 2021). The Controls app below shows why.

How many samples do I need?

Statistical power is the probability that a study will detect an effect (p < 0.05) if the effect is real. Studies with low power often miss real effects, and when they do find something, they tend to overestimate it. A common target is 80% power.

Power depends on four things:

  1. Effect size: the smallest difference that would matter biologically. Decide this from the literature or from what would matter for management, not from what you hope to find.
  2. Variability (SD) of the response. Estimate it from a pilot study, previous studies or similar data.
  3. Sample size (n).
  4. Significance level (α, usually 0.05).

Choose three of them, and you can calculate the fourth. Use the app to see how they trade off.

TipGoing further: power by simulation (click to expand)

For GLMs, mixed models and other complex designs there is no simple formula. Instead, simulate the study many times with your planned sample size and effect, analyse each simulated dataset, and count how often the effect is significant. This is optional, but it is a very powerful way to plan a study.

Example: will 20 plots per treatment detect a 30% increase in fruit number (from a mean of 5 to 6.5 fruits per plant) with a Poisson GLM?

Code 
set.seed(1)
n_per_group <- 20       # plots per treatment
n_sims      <- 500      # number of simulated studies
p_values    <- numeric(n_sims)  # empty vector to store each study's p-value

for (i in 1:n_sims) {
  # 1. Simulate one study
  sim_data <- data.frame(treatment = rep(c("control", "fertilised"), each = n_per_group))
  sim_data$fruits <- rpois(n = 2 * n_per_group,
                           lambda = ifelse(sim_data$treatment == "control", 5, 6.5))
  # 2. Analyse it
  sim_model <- glm(fruits ~ treatment, family = poisson, data = sim_data)
  # 3. Store the p-value for the treatment effect
  p_values[i] <- summary(sim_model)$coefficients["treatmentfertilised", "Pr(>|z|)"]
}

# Power = proportion of simulated studies with p < 0.05
mean(p_values < 0.05)
[1] 0.522

Only about half of the simulated studies detect the effect, so 20 plots per treatment is not enough. Increase n_per_group and run it again to find the sample size that gives 80% power. Real counts are usually overdispersed (Week 8), so you would need even more.

How much survey effort?

For surveys, “sample size” also means effort: how many visits, traps or quadrats.

Detecting a species that is present. If a species is detected with probability p on a single visit when it is present, the chance of missing it on k visits is (1 − p)k. To be 95% sure of detecting it:

\[ k = \frac{\log(1 - 0.95)}{\log(1 - p)} \]

Code 
# Number of visits needed to be 95% sure of detecting a species that is present,
# for single-visit detection probabilities of 0.1 to 0.9
detection <- data.frame(p_detect = seq(0.1, 0.9, by = 0.1)) %>%
  dplyr::mutate(visits_needed = ceiling(log(1 - 0.95) / log(1 - p_detect)))

A cryptic frog detected on 30% of visits needs 9 visits per site before “not detected” reasonably means “absent”. With fewer visits, many of your zeros will be false zeros (see below). Occupancy designs that repeat visits only where a species has not yet been detected can save a lot of effort for rare species (Specht et al., 2017).

Finding most of the species in a community. A species accumulation curve shows how many species you find as you add samples. When the curve flattens, extra samples add few new species.

Zeros and NAs in R

Ecological data are messy, but R doesn’t care: rubbish in, rubbish out. When designing surveys, it is essential to distinguish carefully between “0” and “NA” in your data sheet, because they mean completely different things.

  • Record a 0 only when a site was actually sampled with adequate effort and the species was not detected. Sampling happened, detection was possible, and the count was zero. A zero is data.
  • Record an NA when the site was not sampled, sampling failed, effort was inadequate, or the value is otherwise missing. An NA means “we do not know”.

Mixing them up causes real problems:

  • Coding a missing value as 0 claims a confirmed absence. This biases occupancy or abundance estimates downwards, adds extra zeros and can lead you to fit the wrong model (e.g. a zero-inflated model when the real problem is missing data). If your pH meter broke, the pH of those samples is unknown, not 0.
  • Coding a true zero as NA means R drops that observation from many analyses (e.g. tidyr::drop_na()), reducing your sample size and biasing results.

Zeros can arise in different ways (Blasco-Moreno et al., 2019). In the example below (herbivorous insect species on a plant species), zeros caused by an inexperienced observer (a–b) or by a poor design (c–h) are false zeros, which should be minimised through good design. Structural zeros, caused by the biology of the system (i–k), and random zeros from sampling variation (l) are true zeros.

The scenarios that produce a zero, and how to minimise false zeros:

Most mistakes in published research are not failures of advanced mathematics. Mistaking a change over time for a real treatment effect.

Does mining increase feral cat activity (e.g. because of more human activity)? A before–after, control–impact (BACI) design compares four groups:

  • Control before: the reference (unmined) site before mining started.
  • Control after: the reference site after mining started elsewhere.
  • Impact before: the mine site before mining.
  • Impact after: the mine site after mining.

Cat activity can change over time for reasons that have nothing to do with mining (e.g. season or rainfall). Only the control site tells you how big that background change is.

Code 
viewof bc_eff = Inputs.range([0, 4], {value: 1, step: 0.25, label: "True effect of mining on cat activity"})
viewof bc_drift = Inputs.range([0, 4], {value: 1.5, step: 0.25, label: "Change over time at all sites (e.g. season)"})
viewof bc_n = Inputs.range([5, 50], {value: 20, step: 1, label: "Number of cameras per site"})
viewof bc_seed = Inputs.button("Resample")
Code 
bc = {
  const r = rng(101 + bc_seed);
  const ctrlPre = rnorm(r, bc_n, 10, 2);
  const ctrlPost = ctrlPre.map((v) => v + bc_drift + rnorm(r, 1, 0, 2)[0]);
  const trtPre = rnorm(r, bc_n, 10, 2);
  const trtPost = trtPre.map((v) => v + bc_drift + bc_eff + rnorm(r, 1, 0, 2)[0]);
  const groups = [[ctrlPre, 1, "control"], [ctrlPost, 2, "control"], [trtPre, 4, "impact"], [trtPost, 5, "impact"]];
  const pts = groups.flatMap(([g, x, s]) => g.map((v) => ({x: x + (r() - 0.5) * 0.3, y: v})));
  const ms = groups.map(([g, x, s]) => {
    const m = mean(g), h = qt975(g.length - 1) * sd(g) / Math.sqrt(g.length);
    return {x, m, lo: m - h, hi: m + h, site: s};
  });
  const apparent = ms[3].m - ms[2].m, control = ms[1].m - ms[0].m;
  return {pts, ms, apparent, control, real: apparent - control};
}

How a single outlier, or two groups pooled together, can create a correlation.

A correlation can look real but be created by one extreme point (e.g. a data entry error, Week 1) or by two groups pooled together.

For example, you might study how body mass affects home range size. Without recording sex, lm(home_range ~ mass) shows a positive relationship. But if the species is sexually dimorphic (males are bigger) and males have larger home ranges for other reasons, the relationship within each sex may be flat. Accounting for sex, lm(home_range ~ mass + sex), reveals this.

Code 
viewof fc_type = Inputs.radio(new Map([["A single outlier", "outlier"], ["Two groups pooled together", "groups"]]), {value: "outlier", label: "Cause"})
viewof fc_size = Inputs.range([0, 8], {value: 4, step: 0.5, label: "Distance of the outlier / separation of the groups"})
viewof fc_seed = Inputs.button("Resample")
Code 
fc = {
  const r = rng(40 + fc_seed);
  const X = rnorm(r, 20), Y = rnorm(r, 20);
  let pts;
  if (fc_type === "outlier") {
    pts = X.map((v, i) => ({x: v, y: Y[i], g: "data"}));
    pts.push({x: fc_size, y: fc_size, g: "special"});
  } else {
    pts = X.map((v, i) => {
      const shift = i < 10 ? 0 : fc_size * 0.75;
      return {x: v + shift, y: Y[i] + shift, g: i < 10 ? "data" : "special"};
    });
  }
  const xs = pts.map((d) => d.x), ys = pts.map((d) => d.y);
  const fit = lm1(xs, ys);
  // bootstrap 95% CI for r
  const boots = [];
  for (let b = 0; b < 500; b++) {
    const idx = Array.from({length: xs.length}, () => Math.floor(r() * xs.length));
    const rb = cor(idx.map((i) => xs[i]), idx.map((i) => ys[i]));
    if (Number.isFinite(rb)) boots.push(rb);
  }
  boots.sort((a, b) => a - b);
  const xr = [Math.min(...xs) - 0.5, Math.max(...xs) + 0.5];
  return {pts, r: cor(xs, ys), lo: boots[Math.floor(0.025 * boots.length)], hi: boots[Math.floor(0.975 * boots.length)],
          line: xr.map((x) => ({x, y: fit.b0 + fit.b1 * x}))};
}

A shared cause can create a relationship.

A correlation between two variables does not mean one causes the other. Both may be driven by a third, often unmeasured, variable: a confounder. Once you account for it, the apparent link can disappear.

  • Chocolate and Nobel prizes: across countries, chocolate consumption is correlated with the number of Nobel laureates. Chocolate does not make scientists. Wealthier countries simply have more chocolate and more research funding.
  • Speed and predation: you track animals with GPS and find that faster animals are less likely to be killed by predators (lm(risk ~ speed)). But larger animals are both faster and better at defending themselves. After accounting for body mass (lm(risk ~ speed + mass)), the link between speed and risk disappears. Body mass is the confounder.
Code 
viewof cf_ex = Inputs.radio(new Map([["Chocolate and Nobel prizes", "choc"], ["Speed and predation risk", "pred"]]), {value: "pred", label: "Example"})
viewof cf_str = Inputs.range([0, 0.95], {value: 0.7, step: 0.05, label: "Strength of the confounder"})
viewof cf_adj = Inputs.toggle({label: "Account for the confounder", value: false})
Code 
cf = {
  const r = rng(1010);
  const n = 60, s = cf_str;
  const Z = rnorm(r, n);
  const e1 = rnorm(r, n), e2 = rnorm(r, n);
  const X = Z.map((z, i) => s * z + Math.sqrt(1 - s * s) * e1[i]);
  const sign = cf_ex === "pred" ? -1 : 1;
  const Y = Z.map((z, i) => sign * s * z + Math.sqrt(1 - s * s) * e2[i]);
  const rx = lm1(Z, X).resid, ry = lm1(Z, Y).resid;
  const xs = cf_adj ? rx : X, ys = cf_adj ? ry : Y;
  const fit = lm1(xs, ys);
  const xr = [Math.min(...xs), Math.max(...xs)];
  return {pts: xs.map((x, i) => ({x, y: ys[i]})), r: cor(xs, ys), line: xr.map((x) => ({x, y: fit.b0 + fit.b1 * x}))};
}

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

A non-significant p-value cannot tell the difference between “there is no effect” and “the study was not sensitive enough to detect the effect” (too few independent replicates, too much noise, a poor design). A real effect studied with small samples gives a wide confidence interval that includes zero.

The app simulates five studies of the same real effect with different sample sizes. Report the effect size and its confidence interval, not just the p-value, and plan your sample size in advance (see above).

viewof ns_eff = Inputs.range([0, 1], {value: 0.4, step: 0.05, label: "True effect size"})
viewof ns_seed = Inputs.button("Resample")
Code 
ns = {
  const r = rng(909 + ns_seed);
  const pool = rnorm(r, 100, ns_eff, 1);
  return [5, 10, 20, 50, 100].map((n, i) => {
    const x = pool.slice(0, n), m = mean(x), h = qt975(n - 1) * sd(x) / Math.sqrt(n);
    return {study: `n = ${n}`, m, lo: m - h, hi: m + h, sig: m - h > 0 || m + h < 0 ? "significant" : "not significant"};
  });
}

Recording and reporting

  • Design your data sheet for analysis: one row per observation, one column per variable (Week 1), with a unique ID for every sampling unit and columns for the grouping structure (site, plot, individual, block) and effort (trap nights, area, minutes).
  • Keep a metadata file that explains every column, its units, and how NAs and zeros were recorded.
  • Write methods that someone else could repeat: the sampling unit, sample sizes, the model (response, predictors, distribution, random effects), how assumptions were checked, and the software and packages used (Davis and Kay, 2023; Rowe, 2023).
  • Report results that others can reuse: means, SDs and sample sizes for each group, effect sizes with confidence intervals and exact p-values, even for non-significant results. This lets your study be included in future meta-analyses (Gerstner et al., 2017).
